NISSA: Nested Inlet for Sampling Stratospheric Aerosol

To sample aerosol from a fast aircraft, the inlet has to slow the air down, and slowing air down can create turbulence that knocks particles out of the sample. NISSA is a nested design built to avoid that.

  • Outer section shrouds the sample so the flow is unaffected by the aircraft's angle of attack.
  • Middle section samples isokinetically and slows the flow without adding turbulence.
  • Final section sub-samples the slowed flow and turns it into the aircraft plumbing.

The geometry came out of a parametric CFD study (2D and 3D, near-wall resolution y⁺ ≤ 1, k-ω SST and k-ε). The inlet was then manufactured and flown on the WB-57.

Aspiration efficiency of the PurpleAir PA-II-Flex

Low-cost sensors such as the Plantower PMS5003 report PM1, PM2.5 and PM10, but the housing around them decides which particles actually reach the sensor. I modeled the PA-II-Flex enclosure in 3D and computed how many particles of each size make it through at five wind speeds (0.4, 3, 5, 10 and 20 m/s), with and without turbulent dispersion.

  • 3D model of the housing and sensor slit, k-ε and k-ω SST turbulence models, Fluent discrete random walk particle tracking.
  • Results compared against classical sampling correlations; they do not capture the flow entering this enclosure.
  • Presented at AAAR, with field-data validation as the next step.

Measuring what actually happens inside the inlet

CFD only matters if the real inlet behaves the way the model says. NISSA flew seven research flights on the WB-57 during the SABRE 2025 test campaign, with a miniature hot-wire, absolute and differential pressure taps, and a DPOPS instrument whose size distributions were compared against a balloon sounding of the same air mass.

  • Measured in-flight turbulence intensity of about 19% at steady cruise. A ground wind-tunnel test on the manufactured inlet, with the probe at the flight station, reproduced it (17 to 23%), showing the turbulence is a real flow feature and not an instrument artifact.
  • Built the flight data-reduction pipeline in MATLAB, and calibrated the Teensy 4.1 flight electronics against a laboratory reference chain.
  • Verified that 5 Hz flight sampling preserves mean velocity and turbulence intensity, while being clear that it cannot support spectra.

The flight control and data box

For the WB-57 test flights I built the electronics that ran the hot-wire and pressure measurements. The box has to be reliable, safe to fly, and accurate enough that the numbers it logs can be trusted at altitude.

  • Teensy 4.1 microcontroller reading the sensors and logging to storage.
  • LattePanda single-board computer with a USB hub for control and data handling.
  • ADC for the analog signals, including the output of the hot-wire mini-CTA and its wiring.
  • Power-conditioning boards, a solid-state relay and fuses to run it all from aircraft power.

I calibrated the box against a laboratory reference chain and wrote the MATLAB pipeline that turns its raw logs into velocity and turbulence intensity.

LiDust: an optical dust-emission monitor for industrial stacks

As a Project Associate in the EnERG Lab at IIT Madras (2019 to 2021), I led the design, build and testing of two prototypes of an optical dust-emission monitor, working from concept to finished hardware with a team of engineers and technicians. I was in charge of designing the optical system, a laser with a beam splitter and detector, together with its electronics. The label on the prototype sums up the idea: when other sensors lie, trust an independent optical measurement.

  • Production drawings to ASME GD&T practice in Autodesk Inventor.
  • Field test of the prototype at India's largest thermal power plant (NTPC Vindhyachal).
  • Analyzed isokinetic gravimetric sampling data with time-series signal processing in MATLAB and LabVIEW, the reference the optical method was checked against.
